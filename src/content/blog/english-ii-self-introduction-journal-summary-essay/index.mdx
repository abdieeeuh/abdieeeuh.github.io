## Journal Summary

Topic: Capture the Flag (CTF) in Cybersecurity Education

### Journal 1: The Effectiveness of Capture the Flag as a Network Security Practical Intervention on Students' Self-Efficacy and Learning Outcomes

Author(s): A. St. Khadijah Ridwan, Ummu Radiyah, Muhammad Ihksan Malik, Muhammad Nauval Pattiroi, Muslika, Nining Ulul Fajri, and Nirwana Amir  
Link: [https://journal.unm.ac.id/index.php/INTEC/article/view/11523](https://journal.unm.ac.id/index.php/INTEC/article/view/11523)

APA citation:

Ridwan, A. S. K., Radiyah, U., Malik, M. I., Pattiroi, M. N., Muslika, Fajri, N. U., & Amir, N. (2025). The effectiveness of Capture the Flag as a network security practical intervention on students' self-efficacy and learning outcomes. *Information Technology Education Journal, 4*(4), 811-824. https://doi.org/10.59562/intec.v4i4.11191

Research question/problem statement:

This study asks whether participation in a Capture the Flag (CTF) competition can improve students' self-efficacy and learning outcomes in a network security course when compared with traditional practical exercises. The article addresses the problem that cybersecurity teaching may build technical knowledge but not always strengthen confidence or clearly measure the impact of gamified practice.

Motivation/relevance:

The authors argue that cybersecurity education needs practical and engaging strategies that prepare students for real-world challenges. Because CTF activities are already popular in training environments but their psychological effects are less discussed, the study is relevant for lecturers who want evidence before integrating gamified tasks into the curriculum.

Theoretical framework:

The article is grounded mainly in Bandura's self-efficacy theory and in ideas from gamification and active learning. It assumes that mastery experiences, immediate feedback, competition, and authentic problem solving can increase students' confidence while also improving their understanding of network security concepts.

Method:

The researchers use a quasi-experimental pre-test/post-test control-group design with 60 undergraduate students in a network security course. The experimental group joins a two-week CTF competition, while the control group completes instructor-guided practical exercises. Data come from a self-efficacy scale, a final exam, and a practical skills assessment.

Results/arguments:

The CTF group improves more strongly than the control group on all major measures. Their self-efficacy rises from 3.2 to 4.0, final exam scores reach 85% compared with 75% in the control group, practical skills scores reach 88% compared with 78%, and engagement is also reported as higher.

Conclusions:

The study concludes that CTF competitions are an effective practical intervention for increasing student confidence, conceptual understanding, hands-on skills, and engagement in network security education. At the same time, the authors acknowledge that the small sample and single-institution setting limit how far the findings can be generalized.

Evaluation:

This is a useful and recent academic source because it presents clear hypotheses, measurable outcomes, and direct comparison with a control group. However, the quasi-experimental design, short intervention period, and relatively small sample reduce the strength of its causal claims and limit wider generalization.

Analysis:

This source will be very helpful for supporting an argument that CTF-based learning can improve both performance and self-confidence. I can use it as one of the key empirical studies in the bibliography, especially when discussing how cybersecurity activities affect self-efficacy, engagement, and practical achievement.

### Journal 2: Analysis of Cyber Security Knowledge and Skills for Capture the Flag Competition

Author(s): Jean Tirstan T, Joy Gilbert A, and Nelmiawati  
Link: [https://jurnal.polibatam.ac.id/index.php/JI/article/view/3986](https://jurnal.polibatam.ac.id/index.php/JI/article/view/3986)

APA citation:

Tirstan, J., Gilbert, J., & Nelmiawati. (2022). Analysis of cyber security knowledge and skills for Capture the Flag competition. *Jurnal Integrasi, 14*(1), 14-22. https://doi.org/10.30871/ji.v14i1.3986

Research question/problem statement:

This study investigates what knowledge and technical skills are most needed in CTF competitions and how those findings can be used to design a better learning platform for cybersecurity students. The article addresses the problem that beginners often struggle with platform complexity and do not always receive structured skill development.

Motivation/relevance:

The authors are motivated by the rapid growth of academic CTF competitions and the need to ensure that these competitions genuinely support cybersecurity education. The topic is relevant because it connects CTF design, beginner support, and curriculum planning for student training groups such as the Polibatam Cyber Team.

Theoretical framework:

The paper treats CTF as a form of challenge-based, skill-based, and technique-based learning. It organizes cybersecurity education around major CTF categories such as coding, cryptography, forensics, web, reverse, and pwn, while also emphasizing progressive difficulty levels of easy, medium, and hard.

Method:

The researchers collect and analyze about 500 CTF solutions and write-ups from CTFtime and GitHub, then map the skills and techniques used in each challenge category. They use this analysis to build a CTF platform for PCT regeneration and observe participant progress, performance, and feedback.

Results/arguments:

The study argues that successful CTF training depends on category-specific skill practice, easier platform setup, and gradual difficulty progression. It reports that the skill- and technique-based platform helps around 80% of participants improve, with 40 of 50 students showing progress within one month.

Conclusions:

The article concludes that CTF competitions can significantly support cybersecurity education when they are designed around explicit skills, techniques, and progressive challenge levels. It also suggests that competition is an effective way to motivate learners and build practical competence over time.

Evaluation:

This source is useful because it combines a large collection of CTF write-ups with a practical educational application in a student training platform. However, some parts of the method are described quite generally, and the paper relies more on descriptive analysis and reported improvement than on rigorous experimental comparison.

Analysis:

I can use this source to explain what kinds of knowledge and skills CTF activities develop and why challenge design matters in cybersecurity education. It complements the first journal well because it provides background on skill categories and platform design, while the first journal offers stronger evidence about learning outcomes and self-efficacy.

### Final Essay

Capture The Flag (CTF) competitions are designed to test problem-solving, technical knowledge, and ethical hacking skills in a controlled environment. As these events grow in popularity among schools, universities, and cybersecurity communities, fairness has become a major concern. One modern trend is the use of dynamic flags, where each participant receives a unique flag even when solving the same challenge. This reduces simple flag sharing, yet it does not completely eliminate cheating. Some people argue that dynamic flags are already enough, while others believe stronger detection systems are necessary. I argue that the best cheating detection method in a CTF with dynamic flags is a multi-layered approach combining technical monitoring, behavioral analysis, and human review.

The first reason this method is most effective is that technical monitoring creates reliable evidence. Dynamic flags already help by assigning different answers to different players, making it easier to trace suspicious activity. If two teams submit identical exploit paths, access patterns, or timing sequences, organizers can compare logs to identify whether one team copied from another. For example, the system can record IP addresses, login history, challenge access time, and flag generation data. If a participant opens a challenge, generates a dynamic flag, and moments later another participant submits a related solution without meaningful interaction, the pattern may suggest unauthorized sharing. This type of server-side monitoring is persuasive because it does not depend on rumors or assumptions; it depends on data. In competitive environments, accusations of cheating can damage trust, so organizers need objective proof. Technical logs offer that proof and make enforcement more credible.

A second important idea is that behavioral analysis detects cheating methods that dynamic flags alone cannot stop. Although each player receives a different flag, dishonest participants may still share write-ups, scripts, or step-by-step solutions. In this case, the flag itself is unique, but the learning process is unfairly bypassed. To address this, organizers should analyze unusual behavior such as extremely similar solve times, identical command patterns, or sudden performance jumps. For instance, if several teams that struggled for hours suddenly solve multiple advanced tasks within minutes of one another, that cluster may indicate collusion. Likewise, if a participant repeatedly accesses challenge endpoints in the exact same sequence as another competitor, that repetition may reveal copied methods. Critics may say this approach risks false accusations, but that problem can be reduced by using behavioral analysis as an alert system, not as automatic punishment. Its purpose is to identify suspicious cases for deeper review. Therefore, behavioral analysis strengthens detection because it focuses on how competitors act, not just what they submit.

The third and most persuasive reason is that human review must remain part of the process. Technology can identify patterns, but people are needed to judge context fairly. A strong anti-cheating system should include organizer review of suspicious logs, challenge replays, and even short interviews when necessary. If a team is accused of cheating, they should have a chance to explain their method, show notes, or reproduce part of the solve. This protects honest participants from wrongful punishment while still discouraging dishonest ones. In addition, clear rules should explain what counts as collaboration, flag trading, shared infrastructure abuse, or plagiarism of write-ups. When players know that dynamic flags are backed by active monitoring and case-by-case review, the competition becomes less attractive to cheaters. More importantly, it preserves the educational purpose of CTFs. These events are not only about winning; they are about building cybersecurity skills. A system that catches cheating fairly protects both competition integrity and learning outcomes.

In conclusion, cheating detection in a CTF with dynamic flags should not rely on one tool alone. Dynamic flags are a strong starting point because they reduce direct flag sharing, but they cannot fully stop collusion, copied methods, or unfair assistance. The most effective solution is a multi-layered method: server-side technical monitoring for evidence, behavioral analysis for suspicious patterns, and human review for fair judgment. This approach is persuasive because it balances security with justice. If CTF organizers want competitions to remain credible, educational, and respected, they must go beyond dynamic flags and build a complete system that detects cheating accurately and consistently.
